Most status reports bury the bad news in paragraph four, after three paragraphs establishing that everything is basically fine. The reader has to mine for the thing they actually need to know, and by the time they find it, it reads like it was hidden on purpose — because, functionally, it was.

The format I use now puts risk first, on purpose. Line one is the single sentence a stakeholder needs if they read nothing else. Then what's on track, what's at risk and why, and what I need from someone else to unblock it. No narrative, no hedging.

It felt exposing the first few times — leading with the problem instead of softening into it. But stakeholders trust a report that tells them the bad news early far more than one that reads clean and turns out to have been managing them. Trust compounds. So does the opposite.
