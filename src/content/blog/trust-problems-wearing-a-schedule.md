Early in my career I believed that if a project was slipping, the fix lived somewhere in the plan. Re-sequence the tasks, tighten the estimates, add a buffer, and the dates would come back. Sometimes they did. More often the same project slipped again the following week, in a different place, for a reason the schedule had never predicted.

It took me a few years and a lot of tense status meetings to see the pattern. The plan was rarely the thing that was broken. What was broken was that two people who needed to be honest with each other weren't — and the schedule was simply the place their unspoken problem finally became visible.

## The tell is always the same

When a task quietly moves for the third time with a vague reason attached — "still working through some dependencies" — that's almost never a dependency problem. It's a someone-doesn't-want-to-say-something problem. An engineer who doesn't trust that raising a risk will be met with help instead of blame will manage that risk privately, in the estimate, until it's too big to hide.

So before I touch the timeline, I go looking for the missing conversation. Who's protecting themselves? Who stopped talking to whom? Where did someone learn that honesty here is expensive?

> Fix the trust and the communication, and the timeline tends to follow on its own.

## What I actually do about it

The first move is almost never a process. It's a private, low-stakes conversation with the person who's gone quiet — not to interrogate the slip, but to make it cheap to tell me the truth. **People don't withhold information because they're difficult. They withhold it because, somewhere, it stopped being safe to share.** My whole job in that moment is to make it safe again.

Only once the real blocker is on the table do I go back to the plan — and by then the plan usually reworks itself, because the actual constraint is finally something we can name. The re-sequencing that would have been guesswork an hour ago is now obvious.

## Why this scales

A team that trusts each other surfaces risk early, when it's small and cheap to fix. A team that doesn't surfaces risk late, when it's a crisis. Every framework I've built since — intake, escalation tiers, status reporting — is really just infrastructure for making honesty the fast, safe, default choice. The process isn't the point. The trust the process protects is.

So yes, I still build the Gantt charts. But I've learned to read them as a symptom, not a diagnosis. When the dates won't behave, I don't start with the dates. I start with the people the dates are trying to tell me about.
